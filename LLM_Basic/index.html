    CHAPTER 01 · ACT I — READING THE TEXT

    Tokens: cutting text into pieces

    The question: how can a program that only understands numbers read a sentence?

    A model starts with a fixed vocabulary of pieces — usually 30,000 to 250,000 of them. A tokenizer cuts your text into those pieces, and each piece has an integer ID.

    Common words are a single token. Rare or invented words are assembled from fragments, so the model never meets a word it cannot write down. That is why “unbelievable” can become un · believ · able.

    Rule of thumb in English: one token ≈ ¾ of a word. Remember this — later, limits and prices are counted in tokens, not words.

    Our running sentence becomes 9 tokens. Try it, then edit it.

    CHAPTER 02 · ACT I — READING THE TEXT

    Embeddings: giving the numbers a meaning

    The question: how do we turn an arbitrary ID into something a model can reason with?

    The model keeps a big table with one row per token. Each row is a list of hundreds or thousands of numbers — an embedding. Looking up a token ID returns its row.

    The rows are learned, not designed. Training pushes tokens that behave alike close together, so distance becomes meaning. “Closeness” is usually measured by the angle between two vectors:

    CHAPTER 03 · ACT II — UNDERSTANDING

    Attention: letting words look at each other

    The question: in “…because it was heavy”, what does “it” refer to?

    Humans resolve “it” instantly. A lookup table cannot — “it” has one fixed vector. Attention fixes this: every token asks a question, every other token advertises what it is, and the best matches share their content.

    Each token’s vector is turned into three versions: a query (what am I looking for?), a key (what do I match on?) and a value (what do I hand over?). Match scores between queries and keys become weights; the output is a weighted blend of values.

    Dividing by keeps scores from growing with the dimension, which would make the softmax saturate.

    HOW ONE TOKEN ATTENDS
    vector of “it”, x
    Q = x·WQ“I’m a pronoun. Who is my noun?”
    K = x·WK“I’m a noun, singular, an object.”
    V = x·WV“Here’s what I mean: a ball.”
    Q of “it” · K of every word → scores → softmax → weights × V → new “it”

    FIG. 05 — WQ, WK, WV are learned matrices. The wording in the boxes is a human-readable caricature of what they encode.

    Who is allowed to look at whom?

    Rows are the token doing the looking; columns are what it looks at. Brighter = more attention. Hover a cell, or press the button to watch “it” find “ball”.

    The mask — which cells are allowed to be non-zero — defines the model’s style. Switch patterns to see it. A writer must not peek at future words; a reader can see everything. Chapter 05 builds on this choice.

    CHAPTER 04 · ACT II — UNDERSTANDING

    The transformer: stacking the idea

    The question: one round of mixing is shallow — how do we get depth?

    A transformer block does two things in a row: attention (tokens exchange information) and a feed-forward network (each token is processed on its own, in a wider space). A residual connection adds each step’s input back to its output so information and learning signals can flow through very deep stacks.

    Then the block is repeated — 32, 80, 120 times. Early blocks pick up spelling and grammar; later ones build up facts and reasoning. Click any layer to see what it does.

    Many heads, many views

    Inside the attention step, the vector is split so that several heads run in parallel, each with its own Q, K and V. One head may track the previous word, another may link pronouns to nouns, another may point at the start of the text. Their outputs are glued back together.

    CHAPTER 05 · ACT II — UNDERSTANDING

    Three ways to wire the blocks

    The question: should the model read, write, or turn one text into another?

    The same blocks can be connected in three ways. The difference is only who may look at whom — the mask from chapter 03.

    Encoder-only models see the whole text at once and output one vector per token. Decoder-only models see only the past and generate one token at a time. Encoder-decoder models read a source with an encoder, then write a target with a decoder that looks back at the source (cross-attention).

    CHAPTER 06 · ACT III — LEARNING

    Pretraining: where the knowledge comes from

    The question: the shape is built, but every number is random. How do they get their values?

    With a random model, the guess for “…because it was ___” is noise. Training fixes that with one simple game, played trillions of times on text from books, websites and code:

    1. Hide the next token. 2. Let the model guess. 3. Measure how little probability it gave the true token — the loss. 4. Nudge every parameter slightly so that guess would have been better (backpropagation).

    To predict the next word well across all that text, the model has to pick up grammar, facts and patterns of reasoning. Nobody programs them in; they are the cheapest way to lower the loss.

    That is training. Afterwards the numbers are frozen; using the model is called inference. Toggle between the two and watch what moves.

    CHAPTER 07 · ACT III — LEARNING

    Tuning: from text-continuer to assistant

    The question: a base model finishes text. How does it learn to answer?

    Ask a base model “What is the capital of France?” and it may continue with more quiz questions — that is the likeliest web text. To make an assistant, the model passes through further stages. Click each one.

    Pretraining costs the vast majority of the compute. Everything after it uses far less data but shapes how the model behaves.

    Learning from preferences

    How do you teach “helpful” when you cannot write it as a formula? You show people two answers and ask which is better. A reward model learns to predict those votes, and the assistant is then optimised toward answers that score well (RLHF, or the simpler DPO).

    Be the human: vote on two pairs.

    INTERACTIVE · BE THE LABELLER

    Fine-tuning: changing the default voice

    Fine-tuning continues training on a small, focused dataset so the model’s defaults change — tone, format, domain vocabulary. It teaches behaviour more than facts.

    Flip the switch: a tiny bakery-support dataset flows into the same base model, and the answer to an identical question changes style. Cheap variants such as LoRA train a small add-on instead of rewriting every weight.

    CHAPTER 08 · ACT IV — RUNNING

    Decoding: choosing the next word

    The question: the model outputs scores, not words. What happens when you press enter?

    The model outputs one raw score, a logit, for every word in its vocabulary. Softmax turns scores into probabilities. Then one token is sampled, appended to the text, and the whole thing repeats — until a stop token appears or max tokens is reached.

    Temperature reshapes the odds: low is predictable, high is adventurous. Top-p throws away the unlikely tail before sampling. Click Sample token repeatedly: each pick feeds the next step, just like a real model.

    CHAPTER 09 · ACT IV — RUNNING

    Context: the working memory

    The question: the loop keeps appending tokens. Where do they all go?

    Every step of that loop re-reads the whole text so far. A model can only hold a fixed number of tokens at once — its context window. Your instructions, the conversation and the model’s own reply all share it.

    Go past it and the oldest tokens fall off. The model does not warn you — it simply cannot see them any more. Cost scales with tokens too, so the window is a billing line as well as a spec.

    CHAPTER 10 · ACT IV — RUNNING

    Scale: why bigger keeps working, and what it costs

    The question: how big should a model be, and what does it take to run one?

    Three dials control a model: parameters (how many numbers), data (how many tokens it trains on) and compute (how much arithmetic). Remarkably, loss falls along a smooth curve as these grow together — a scaling law.

    The floor is the unavoidable randomness of language. Each ×10 of compute still helps, but by less and less. Because the curve is so regular, labs forecast big runs from small ones.

    CHAPTER 11 · ACT V — USING

    Using LLMs well

    The question: now that you know what is inside, how do you choose and use one wisely?

    Open or closed?

    Closed models are rented through an API: strong quality, zero ops, but your text leaves your machine. Open weights models ship the parameters — you can run them on your own hardware (chapter 10 told you what that takes), inspect and tune them.

    Scenario clinic

    Scenario A — the privacy-sensitive app

    A hospital wants an assistant that summarises clinical notes. Legal forbids patient text from reaching any third-party server. What fits, and why?

    Hint: trace the data. When you call a closed API, where do the tokens physically travel?
    An open-weights model running locally. The weights sit on hospital hardware, so notes never leave the building. Chapter 10 told you what hardware that takes — a quantised mid-size model fits on a single GPU.

    Scenario B — the amnesiac chatbot

    “I told it my name in the first message, twenty minutes ago. Now it says it doesn’t know. Why?”

    Hint: the model has no memory — only a window. What happens when the conversation outgrows it?
    The conversation exceeded the context window (chapter 09). The earliest tokens fell out, so the model literally cannot see them. Repeat key facts, summarise the chat so far, or use a longer-context model.

    Scenario C — same prompt, different answers

    You send exactly the same prompt twice and get two different replies. Is the model broken?

    Hint: what does the model output at each step — one word, or a distribution?
    No. Each step samples from a probability distribution (chapter 08). With temperature above zero, different tokens can be picked, and early differences compound. Lower the temperature for repeatable output.
